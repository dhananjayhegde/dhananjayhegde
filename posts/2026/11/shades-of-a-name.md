I was given two names at birth.  One by my father, the other by my amternal aunts and cousins.  So, a line was drawn between my paternal and maternal relatives based on how I was called.  So much so that whenever someone asked me my name, I would answer “at home, I am Dhananjay, but at my granny’s home, I am Chinmay”.

This is how it happened, as my parents and cousins have fondly recounted over the years - 

I was born in the pre-internet era. Thank God for that! Which meant I was saved from this wild search for names on the internet for a weird amalgamation of Indian, Sanskritic, Greek and Latin names with supposedly profound meanings.  When the time came and Purohit asked what I should be named, my father told him it would be “Dhananjay”.  

It seems my aunts and cousins were very upset with this decision as they did not agree with the name at all.  Some even became teary and adamant at this point. Reason was this - around that time, there happened to be an infamous criminal case on a person named “Dhananjay”.   My older cousins (mother’s elder sister’s children) and my aunts considered that name a bad omen.  Their reasoning was simple - "when there are hundreds of nice names, why go for something like that". I completely agree with them! After much hue and cry, they apparently said they would call me “Chinmay”.  The matter was thus settled and I became “Dhananjay at home, Chinmay at granny’s”.  

My name and how I am called has taken different shapes over the years.  My parents, as I remember, have never called me by my name! It's not even close to the name that they gave me.  Then as well as now, they simply call me "Tamma" ( ತಮ್ಮ - a kannada word for younger brother!).  I cannot blame them for this.  This is how most parents call their children in my hometown - boys get called Tamma and girls get called Tangi (ತಂಗಿ - younger sister).

During my childhood days, as was the norm then, younger kids in the village would call me by adding the suffix “Anna” (an elder brother) to my name.  So it would become “Dhananjay Anna”.  But the name was too long and hard for them to pronounce. Especially the heavy aspirations of the letter "Dha" (ಧ).  So, it often got dropped for the much softer sound “Janjanna” (ಜಂಜಣ್ಣ) -  sound you hear when you shake your pocket with a bunch coins in it.  

Situation was much better on the maternal side though.  Younger cousins would call me “Chinnanna” (ಚಿನ್ನಣ್ಣ).   This nama gave the least chance to anyone to deform.  To be honest, there were times I wondered why my name was not just Chinmay.  It sounded very nice when my grandmother or maternal aunts called me “Chinnu”.  It sounded sweet.  “Dhananjay” could never sound that way.  The name felt very serious. 

I went to a place called VajraLLi (ವಜ್ರಳ್ಳಿ) for High School.  On the first day of the school when someone called out “Dhananjay”, two heads turned.  The moment my classmates realized the confusion, they decided both Dhananjays would be known only by our initials.  So, I became DC (Dhananjay Chandrashekhar) and the other one became RD (Dhananjay Ramachandra).  This name stuck to me like Fevicol to wood.  Even some of the teachers started calling "DC".  Even today, after 25 years, my classmates from Highschool address me by that name.  

I have been called by different names over the years or by different forms of my name.  Dhanu, DJ and so on.  But, I am grateful that my name hasn’t been malformed or deformed so much as some other’s.  

For instance, growing up, in my village, my neighbours were a combined family.  There was an older boy in the house called “Venkataramana”.  Since the name was too long, he became “Venkatramana”, “Venkati”, “Yenkati” and when people felt very affectionate, he became “Venkatu” and so on.  We, being younger to him, would call him “Yenkatannayya” (ಯೆಂಕಟಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ) or just “Entannayya” (ಎಂಟಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ) - "8  - brother".  It's not uncommon for a beautiful name such as Lakshminarayana to become "Lakku",  or Rajeswari to become "Raju", or "Suryanarayana" to become "Suri".  Ganapati often becomes Gappati, or even "Gappu" when the caller has a lot of affection to express.  My mother Savitri is called "Saitri" (ಸೈತ್ರಿ) by most people.

Once the kinship words start getting attached to these shortened names, it gets even crazy.  When Suryanarayana or Lakshminarayana become grandfathers, they simply get called "Surajja" or "Lakkajja" (Ajja - ಅಜ್ಜ - for grandfather).  "Appachhi" (ಅಪ್ಪಚ್ಚಿ) is a way to call father's younger brother or mother's younger sister's husband.  So, Lakashminarayana can become "Lakkappachhi" (ಲಕ್ಕಪ್ಪಚ್ಚಿ).  This has so many double sounds, when you say it out loud, it feels less like calling someone and more like testing a drum kit.

Even common nouns such as "Annayya" (ಅಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ) and "Akkayya" (ಅಕ್ಕಯ್ಯ) - endearing ways of calling "Anna" (ಅಣ್ಣ - elder brother) and "Akka" (ಅಕ್ಕ - elder sister) becomes "Anya" (ಅಣ್ಯ) and "Akya" (ಅಕ್ಯ).  What if you had 2 elder brothers? What would you call the younger one then? Because he is younger/smaller, so to speak, of the elder brothers, he would become "sanna" (ಸಣ್ಣ- small) "Annayya" (ಅಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ).  But you cannot give a space on between when calling.  So he would become "Sannannayya" (ಸಣ್ಣಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ) which then becomes "Shannannayya" (ಶಣ್ಣಣ್ಣಯ್ಯ).  But tell me, who has so much time? So, he often becomes just "Shanya" (ಶಣ್ಯ).  

So, some times I wonder, do people even like calling names longer than 2 syllabals?  If not, why even bother with such long names at all?

Despite all these, I think, the names that people call you with start growing on you over a period of time.  May be, they sound strange initially.  But, after a while, if they call you with your actuual name or a different one suddenly, you wonder "what happend? Is everything OK?".  When my maternal cousins some times call me with my full name "Dhananjaya" it feels a bit strange.  "Has the affection faded away?". Since most of the time my name is called with its Hindi version "Dhananjay", which is how even I have come to use it now,  when someone calls me with the final "Schwa" sound "Dhananjaya", it feels strangely nice too.
